# What Do IT Certifications Actually Certify?

Have you ever said, heard, or related to the statement below?

> “I just need to earn an AWS certification, and I will automatically stand out on the team and get promoted by my manager.”

Or perhaps this one?

> “Certifications are a waste of time. If I show I am qualified on the job, I will be valued.”

Just as the difference between medicine and poison is the dose, I would like to share my view on why IT certifications can be highly relevant to our careers when approached with the right mindset.

The certification wave has taken over the job market, hiring processes, email signatures, and LinkedIn posts—usually to a great and positive response.

But why is everyone pursuing a certification? The less romanticized, but most realistic, answer is that we all want a competitive advantage and want to remain relevant in the IT market, especially after the recent startup bubble burst.

But how do you become competitive? How do you make those contributions? It is not simply about passing an exam that says you are a cloud practitioner. It is much more about the results you deliver for your employers. And remember: collaboration, alignment with company culture, and ethical conduct are also essential to being recognized. Delivering results at everyone else's expense is not the answer.

And there is no secret to “getting there”—I use quotation marks because there is no finish line in IT. Study, stay current, practice, deliver professional and personal projects, then repeat from the beginning.

That is where certifications hold their greatest value for me: the learning journey enables you to make increasingly meaningful contributions and better understand the context you are working in. Passing the exam is a consequence.

When I took the AWS Certified Cloud Practitioner exam, I was very nervous. People around me created an atmosphere of “you have already passed.” They all meant well and wanted to encourage me, but it tripled the pressure I put on myself and my fear of failing the exam.

The nearly 40 hours I dedicated to studying, practice exams, and projects paid off in my day-to-day work. This is considered AWS's least complex exam, but I took a conservative approach because of that unspoken pressure to pass. I was able to have higher-level discussions with my team about a range of AWS solutions and contribute more than ever to decisions involving S3 bucket object lifecycles, SQS/SNS messaging solutions, instance selection based on compute and storage needs and cost optimization, and a committee discussion about replacing an Internet Gateway with a NAT Gateway in subnets, among other topics.

Even if I had not passed, it would still have been worth it despite the disappointment. The contributions I have been able to make since then helped me understand that a certification is not an end in itself; it is a way to gain more knowledge and contribute more, and more effectively.

In short, the greatest value of a certification is not the destination, but the journey. Passing the exam simply gives that journey legitimacy and proof.

Study hard, build personal projects using the AWS Free Tier, exchange ideas with your peers and leaders, and remember that all this studying and certification work should help us contribute more to results.

I dedicate this post to my direct reports, who embraced this challenge with me. More than 80% of our leadership group is certified—and qualified, delivering great work!
