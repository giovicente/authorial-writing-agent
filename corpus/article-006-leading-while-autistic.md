# Leading While Being on the Autism Spectrum

“Manager” and “autistic” may not be two words that naturally come to mind when placed in the same sentence.

Management involves communication, influence, reading context, conflict, ambiguity, and, whether we like it or not, a fair amount of organizational politics. Some of those things can represent meaningful challenges for someone on the spectrum.

And yet, I have been a leader since I was 29.

At 34, I was diagnosed with twice-exceptionality: giftedness and autism spectrum disorder, Level 1 support needs.

In other words: I was already leading before I knew.

The diagnosis did not create strengths that were not already there, nor did it invent new difficulties. It gave me names, context, and, most importantly, a better way to understand why some situations had always felt very natural to me while others required a disproportionate amount of effort.

And that may be the main reflection I have today about leading while being on the spectrum.

> “Is autism incompatible with management?”

My experience says no.

In fact, some of the traits I now understand more clearly have been important to my growth as a manager.

I am very comfortable with metrics, indicators, planning, and strategy. I like breaking large problems into smaller parts, understanding cause-and-effect relationships, tracking execution, and creating mechanisms that make it easier to see whether we are actually moving forward.

I also believe I am a good active listener.

When someone brings me a problem, my natural tendency is to understand the facts, separate what actually happened from the interpretations around it, and reach the fairest judgment I can.

There is an interesting side to that as well.

Organizational politics exist. Human relationships involve interests, perceptions, alliances, and disputes that are not always explicit.

My first instinct, however, is usually to analyze what is objectively happening.

In many situations, that is a major advantage.

In others, it means I need to complement my own reading with context, experience, and people I trust.

## The General Should Have Been a Soldier Once

Another important part of my own case is giftedness.

My career has always been closely connected to engineering itself: programming, architecture, distributed systems, problem analysis, and solution design.

That continues to be a valuable tool after moving into leadership.

I do not believe a technical manager necessarily needs to be the person with the deepest technical knowledge on the team — in fact, building a team where that is always true would probably be a bad sign — but having enough background to take part in technical discussions, understand risks, ask good questions, and occasionally unblock situations makes a real difference.

I often think that the general should have been a soldier once.

Not because the general needs to keep doing every task the soldiers do, but because knowing the terrain changes the quality of the decisions you make.

That combination of structured thinking, deep interest in specific subjects, and technical background helps me guide the team and keep delivery moving.

But, as with any leadership profile, some characteristics help while others need to be worked on deliberately.

## Not Everything Happens Intuitively

There are aspects of leadership that require more intentional effort from me.

Executive communication is one of them.

Something can be technically clear or operationally under control without necessarily being well communicated to every audience.

Learning how to adapt the message, context, timing, and level of detail is a skill I have had to develop consciously — and still do.

The same applies to situations where multiple interpretations are possible.

Not every decision depends only on explicit facts. There is context, perception, history, and interpersonal dynamics.

Recognizing that helped me understand something important: what does not happen naturally can often be supported by systems.

## Systems Are My Allies

I really like the idea of creating explicit mechanisms for things that other people may do intuitively.

If critical information needs to circulate, I can create explicit communication checkpoints.

If an important situation requires a fast response, I can define criteria beforehand.

I often think about Civil Defense emergency protocols.

When an emergency happens, that is probably not the best time to figure out from scratch who needs to be notified, which decisions need to be made, or in what order actions should happen.

A large part of that can already be defined in advance.

I have been trying to apply a similar logic to leadership.

Visible roadmaps, regular follow-ups, clear escalation criteria, decision records, communication routines, and checklists for critical situations.

Maybe some people do part of this intuitively.

I prefer to structure it.

And that is fine.

The outcome matters much more than the mental mechanism used to get there.

## Have People You Trust

There is still one thing no system can fully replace: complementarity.

Having people you trust around you is extremely valuable.

People who interpret situations differently can broaden your perspective, challenge assumptions, and help you see angles that might not have been your first reading.

That does not mean outsourcing judgment.

It means recognizing that all of us have blind spots.

Having someone who naturally acts as a second-in-command can help a great deal. Someone you can use to validate perceptions, discuss context, and build a fuller interpretation before making certain decisions.

Come to think of it, this is not even advice exclusive to neurodivergent managers.

Even neurotypical managers need a good sidekick.

Maybe being atypical simply makes that need more explicit.

## Should You Tell People?

This is a deeply personal decision.

Receiving a diagnosis does not mean everyone in the company automatically needs to know about it.

At the same time, I do not think it is healthy to treat the condition as something that must be hidden out of shame.

Writing this article, in fact, is a deliberate choice to share part of that experience.

I am comfortable talking about aspects of how I function, the strengths I recognize in myself, and the areas I still need to develop.

But transparency does not mean unlimited exposure.

Authenticity does not require every piece of personal information to be available to everyone, in every context.

Choosing what to share, with whom, and at what moment is also part of self-awareness.

To me, there is an important difference between publicly acknowledging that I have areas to develop and turning those areas into a detailed description of personal vulnerabilities.

The first can be healthy.

The second is not always necessary.

Sharing a diagnosis can be an act of transparency. It does not have to become a declaration of incapacity, fragility, or a request for the professional bar to disappear.

I am still responsible for my results.

I still need to improve the things I struggle with.

The diagnosis simply allows me to work with those differences much more consciously.

## So, Can Autistic People Be Good Managers?

Of course.

Just as they can be good software engineers, doctors, teachers, artists, entrepreneurs, or anything else for which they have interest, opportunity, and the chance to develop.

I also do not believe there is such a thing as “the autistic manager.”

The spectrum is, after all, a spectrum.

The characteristics I described here are mine. Another autistic person may have completely different strengths and challenges.

What I can say from my own experience is that understanding how I function has not reduced my professional ambition.

Quite the opposite.

Today, I have a better understanding of which characteristics I should leverage and which ones I need to work on consciously.

In some areas, my differences strengthen my leadership.

In others, they require systems, practice, and help from people I trust.

Maybe this principle is much less specific than it first appears.

Regardless of diagnosis, every manager should be able to answer a few difficult questions about themselves:

In which situations do I naturally make good decisions?

Where do I need to be more intentional?

What mechanisms help me work better?

What kind of person complements my style?

And who do I trust enough to show me perspectives I might not see on my own?

My diagnosis answered some of those questions.

The others remain part of the work I do every day.
