# The Mythical Man-Month and Timeless Lessons in Software Engineering

Recently, to reinforce my foundations, I reread a well-known software engineering book that offers a range of recommendations and best practices for managing software development projects.

It covers topics such as communication, definitions of done, team size, value delivery, and development and testing best practices.

> “Which Agile Practices book did you read? Is it preparation for a certification?”

Actually, the book was first published in 1975—46 years before this article was written (!!!). It is *The Mythical Man-Month*, written by Frederick P. Brooks Jr., an American computer scientist, engineer, and professor.

As its cover puts it, the book is a collection of essays on software engineering for professionals who want to understand—and avoid—the traps that contribute to project failure.

What caught my attention was how it addresses subjects that remain highly relevant today. Many have even been absorbed into Agile methodologies and have helped shape modern—or are they really so modern?—development practices. The following are especially worth highlighting:

- **Adding people to a late project makes it later.** People and months are not interchangeable. This happens because of the learning curve and the time required for new team members to gain context, break down work, and create additional communication channels.
- **Communication is critical.** Talk to your peers and customers. Here, “customer” can mean not only the user representative, but also the developer building an integration with your API. Pick up the phone, schedule project status meetings—or the famous daily stand-ups—send a smoke signal, message them on Teams…
- **Conceptual integrity matters most.** Defining what should be built is harder than building it. There is no point in doing a great job on something that should not have been built in the first place. Recent and acclaimed books such as Marty Cagan's *Inspired* and *Empowered*—both highly recommended—discuss the importance of building products that create a competitive advantage and delight customers. Conceptual integrity and product vision are the seeds of that mindset.
- **Build “surgical” teams:** small, cross-functional teams focused on a single domain. Sounds a bit like the squad model, does it not?
- **Change is constant.** Learn to deal with it, and make “discard” part of your everyday vocabulary. It is the *fail fast and learn faster* of decades ago.
- **Define project deliverables clearly** and make a plan to achieve them.
- **How does a project fall a year behind? One day at a time.** The warning was already there in 1975.
- **Refine requirements with a top-down approach.** Start with the business need and work your way down, detailing implementation and understanding as questions at the higher levels are clarified.
- **Do not build software; grow software.** Deliver value incrementally, modularize, and run regression and smoke tests with every module added. The book recommends this approach without using these names—the techniques had not yet been “named” at the time. Software will grow, and maintenance is a constant.
- **There is no silver bullet.** A software system has essential characteristics that make magical solutions very unlikely: complexity, conformity, changeability, and invisibility.

I strongly recommend reading it. Very few points have aged poorly—although, of course, some have—and even those are interesting because they show which software development challenges from the 1960s and 1970s have already been overcome.

It is a highly relevant read for getting back to basics and reflecting on the timeless challenges of software engineering. It also provides a strong foundation for engaging with more modern material.
