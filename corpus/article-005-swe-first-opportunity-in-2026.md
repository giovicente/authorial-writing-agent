# Why Is It So Hard to Land a First Software Engineering Opportunity? (Plus Some Advice)

Recently, I spoke with two people who work in IT support at my current employer. It was only a brief conversation, but I could tell they were smart, diligent, and hardworking young people. They spoke about wanting to transition into software engineering and programming, but had run into barriers such as a lack of opportunities and uncertainty about how to break through that initial barrier.

I told them that, in nearly 14 years of paid and unpaid work across programming, teaching, and technical leadership, I have never seen the runway be as hostile to newcomers as it is today.

That conversation, along with a few personal projects, motivated me to write this article. I hope it offers a clearer view of how I see this moment and how to adapt to it. As always, all opinions are my own.

> “With all these AI tools, do we still need junior engineers?”

The short answer is yes, we do.

To expand on that: every serious company and manager must think about the efficient allocation of people—and, more recently, agents—as well as succession planning. Today's senior engineer, tech lead, or manager was a junior engineer yesterday. Senior professionals will retire, and teams experience attrition. We need to bring in and prepare the future engineers who will build and maintain the platforms and systems that are now a company's primary non-human assets.

The more important change may be this: although we still need junior engineers, the profile of a junior engineer now needs to be different.

Not long ago, we went through a period of abundance. There were plenty of openings, and bootcamps proliferated. That brought many excellent professionals into the market—I taught at a bootcamp, and I am proud to see many former students thriving on their own merits. But there was virtually no friction or barrier to entry. On one hand, great professionals received opportunities they deserved; on the other, many people entered the field after building a standard, ready-made project and did not pursue deeper learning afterward.

Now, in addition to the shortage of openings, the bar has risen significantly. AI will know code syntax better than we do and can produce convincing output. Knowing a language's commands and basic logic is no longer enough, as it once was.

The market absorbed many people during that growth period and then slowed down. There are far more candidates competing for entry-level positions, while AI tools have increased the productivity of professionals who already have experience.

> “So what is expected of a junior engineer if AI now writes code for us?”

AI can produce convincing, high-quality code in record time. Tools such as Claude and Codex are much sharper at code syntax than most of us. What AI still cannot do on its own is define the most appropriate approach to solving a problem—unless that has been made explicit in the prompts and specifications.

AI can certainly suggest excellent technical approaches, but the quality of the decision still depends on the quality of the context provided and on a human's ability to evaluate the trade-offs.

If you do not specify *how* you want something done, AI will do it its own way. And if that approach is not ideal for the problem—and you lack the background to review and identify that—you will be creating work for “vibe-code cleanup specialists.”

When it comes to AI, the rule is clear: *garbage in, garbage out*. If I ask in a suboptimal way, the output will be suboptimal too—though it may still look convincing, which is dangerous. This is the key point for me: the “modern junior engineer” needs, from an early stage, to be more passionate about gaining a solid understanding of the problem than about impulsively proposing solutions or typing memorized algorithms. Understanding the problem is crucial, but the goal is to solve it in the best possible way—and that requires the range of knowledge I mentioned earlier.

> “What exactly is that range of knowledge?”

It is essentially the foundations of our profession: the “boring but important” material. The list is not exhaustive, but it includes algorithms and data structures, software testing, system design, and DevOps.

The goal is not to memorize, but to learn: to know when a cache solves or worsens a problem; to understand which sorting algorithm best fits a given context and data organization; and to understand clean-code conventions, design principles, and software architecture.

The counterintuitive part is that you need to implement these structures by hand, work through lots of exercises, and build personal projects so you can internalize them and know how to ask AI for them. You need to separate training from game time. During game time, an increasingly large share of code will probably be produced or mediated by AI.

Another important point: show genuine interest in the product you will work on. The market has greatly reduced the space for people whose role is merely to turn an explicit requirement into code; Claude and similar tools can now take on much of that role. That is the reality in many companies, and it affects those who chose this profession because they love the flow state that comes from writing code by hand.

However, it does not matter if AI produces the most optimized code in the world if it does not truly solve the customer's problem. Understanding not only *what* is done and *how*, but also *why*, is crucial right now. It takes you beyond entering commands and makes you a professional who can engage effectively with other areas of the business. Product knowledge is no longer just a concern for product owners and managers—if it was ever exclusively theirs at all.

Finally, look for references and understand the constraints experienced programmers place on the AI development lifecycle and how they make their requests. Read their `CLAUDE.md` and `AGENTS.md` files, note terms you do not know so you can study them later, and try applying those ideas in personal projects.

One extra tip: ask lots of questions, including during interviews. This foundation is not meant to give someone every answer; it is meant to help them ask better questions, both of AI and of human colleagues. A junior role is still an entry point, and there is much more to learn from these foundations.

> “Is any of this useful if there are no openings?”

The points above do not solve the scarcity of jobs and opportunities. But precisely because opportunities are scarce, the bar for the positions that do appear is high and the competition is fierce. If it will unfortunately already be difficult with this foundation, it will be harder without it.

## TL;DR

1. Study the fundamentals of computing and software engineering.
2. Show interest in the product. If you are a customer of the company interviewing you, mention it and discuss its app or website thoughtfully and critically.
3. See how experienced people are using AI and study the concepts they place into their agents.
4. Remember that a junior role is an entry point, and that junior engineers do need the support of more senior people. The much-discussed “range of knowledge” also helps you ask better questions and accelerate your learning curve and delivery.

This article is based entirely on personal observations and is not intended as absolute truth. Ironically, it was not produced with AI. Comments are always welcome.
